YouTube Kids alternatives for older children

What to do when a seven-to-twelve-year-old finds YouTube Kids babyish, but you are not ready to hand over the whole of YouTube.

Last updated 14 August 2026

There is a specific moment most parents hit, usually somewhere between seven and nine. The child stops accepting YouTube Kids. Not because of the controls — because of the catalogue. Their friends talk about creators who aren't there. The interface looks like it was made for a five-year-old, and they know it.

At that point the honest options are narrower than the internet suggests. Here they are, with what each actually costs you.

What actually goes wrong at this age

It is worth being precise, because the risks change as children get older and most advice is written for toddlers.

  • The recommendation sidebar, not search. Children this age mostly do not search for anything troubling. They click what appears next. Autoplay and recommendations are where drift happens.
  • Comments. Under a perfectly ordinary gaming video, the comments are frequently the least suitable thing on the page.
  • Content that is fine for fifteen, not for nine. Most of what parents object to here is not banned material. It is horror-adjacent thumbnails, casual profanity and mature humour — all entirely within YouTube's rules.
  • Volume. A nine-year-old can watch forty short videos in an hour. No parent reviews forty videos.

A reasonable default

If you want one recommendation rather than a menu: turn off autoplay, hide or disable comments, and set a daily time limit. Those three cost nothing, take five minutes, and address the three biggest problems above. Do that before installing anything.

If your child still needs the real catalogue and you want a per-video check on top, that is the point at which an app earns its place.

Common questions

Should I just let them have normal YouTube?

Eventually, yes — every child gets there. The useful question is whether the jump happens now, at once, or gradually. Most families do better with a staged handover than with a single switch.

What about banning it entirely?

It rarely holds past this age. Children with a blanket ban usually find access somewhere less supervised, which is a worse outcome than a filtered version at home.

Do these controls work on a school-issued device?

Often not. Managed school devices carry their own profiles and may block third-party controls outright. Check with the school before assuming coverage.
